02

What the two days are about

Social network analysis is usually taught as a software course. Participants learn to produce a picture before they can say what the picture asserts, and the picture is persuasive out of all proportion to the evidence behind it. This bootcamp inverts that. Every quantity is counted by hand on networks small enough to hold in view, and the analytic work is the argument about what should count as a node and what should count as a tie.

Day 1

What counts as a tie

Nodes, ties and the adjacency matrix, built by hand. Sixteen Florentine families worked two different ways. Then your own group maps a small social world it shares, and commits to a sealed prediction about it.

Overnight

The only computation

The convenor turns every group's paper sheet into a laid-out diagram with degree and betweenness. That is the only time a computer touches anything all weekend.

Day 2

What structure does

Predictions opened against the machine-drawn version. Then the Indian Ocean as a knowledge network, Randall Collins on intellectual lineages, horizons of understanding, and the shift to symptom networks.

By the end, each participant can

  • say, for any network diagram, what the nodes are, what the edges assert and how the boundary was drawn;
  • build an adjacency matrix from a verbal description and compute degree, density and path length by hand;
  • explain why two tie rules over the same population give different answers about who is central;
  • tell an observed tie, an inferred tie and an estimated statistical association apart;
  • write a one-page network memo for a problem in their own field.

What you do not need

No laptop. There is no software in this bootcamp at all. No coding, on either day. No maths beyond counting. No reading beforehand — the reading list comes afterwards and is optional. No prior anything: we assume you have never heard the word “node”.

Two rules held throughout

Every metric is counted by hand before any machine computes it. And no more than five new terms per session, all of them on a printed glossary card handed out in the first ten minutes. Nothing is taught that is not on that card.

05

Access, and how the group work runs

The room

  • ·Leave any session at any time. No need to say why, ask permission, or explain on returning.
  • ·A quiet room next door for both days. Nobody will follow you into it.
  • ·Breaks are at fixed times and we do not run over into them.
  • ·Two sessions are genuinely loud — Saturday and Sunday 11:15, both flagged in the brochure. Bring ear defenders or loops if you use them.
  • ·Standard overhead lighting. Say so in advance if that is a problem and we will dim the room or seat you away from it.
  • ·Nothing is timed, graded or recorded. There is no test.

Groups, and the data you generate

We assign the groups. Three or four people, the same group both days, seated at registration. Tell us on the form if you are coming with someone you know.

You never have to be one of the dots. On Saturday afternoon your group maps a social world. If you would rather not be in it, say so and you still do every other part; this is announced to the whole room so declining is not conspicuous.

The questions are deliberately dull. Only who has studied or worked with whom. Nothing about liking, conflict, money or health. People outside the room appear as A1, A2, A3; real names never leave your table, and the key sheet is destroyed before anything is handed in. Submitted data is deleted within seven days.

Session 6 is announced before it starts. It uses published research on mental health, including personality disorders, as an example of the third kind of edge. Nobody rates themselves or anyone else on anything, and you are free to step out for that part.
